1. Hook: picture a crowded city street around the 1920s. Pushcarts, a bakery with bread in the window, cheese hanging in a shop, a pot on every stove. Most Italian immigrants of that time came from the south of Italy and had very little. What they cooked was cheap, clever and delicious. Promise: a walk through that kitchen, the dishes, the shopping and the habits, and what we can still cook from it today.
2. Part 1 — The shopping street: pushcart markets for vegetables and fruit; the bakery for bread every day; the salumeria for cheese, cured meats, olives and dried pasta; the fish seller on Fridays. Keep it general (many cities had neighborhoods like this), no specific street names or dates.
3. Part 2 — Bread and beans, the everyday base (5): pasta e fagioli; escarole and beans; lentil soup; minestra of greens and whatever was left; bread used to the last crumb (bread soup, toasted breadcrumbs on pasta).
4. Part 3 — Cheap cuts, big flavor (4): braciole rolled and simmered in the Sunday sauce; sausage made or bought from the neighborhood butcher; tripe and other inexpensive cuts that needed time; meatballs stretched with bread. Many families could afford more meat in America than they had back home; say this as a commonly told part of the story, not a statistic.
5. Mid-video: the free 25 Rules for Eating Out is the first link in the description.
6. Part 4 — The Sunday pot: the long-simmered tomato sauce with meat that fed the whole family after church, the first course of pasta and the meat after. Point to Sal's free Sunday Marinara recipe as the place to start.
7. Part 5 — Sandwiches and work food (3): peppers and eggs on bread; sausage and peppers at the neighborhood street feasts for patron saints; a frittata of leftovers wrapped in bread for lunch.
8. Part 6 — Fridays and holidays (3): fish on Fridays; baccalà (salt cod) soaked for days; the Christmas Eve seafood dinner that became the Feast of the Seven Fishes tradition for many families, with numbers that vary by family.
9. Part 7 — Home habits (5): a small garden or pots of tomatoes, basil and peppers wherever there was room; canning tomatoes at the end of summer; wine made at home by many families; nothing wasted (rinds into soup, stale bread into breadcrumbs); everyone at the table on Sunday.
10. Part 8 — Sweets for special days (3): pizzelle; struffoli in honey at Christmas; ricotta-based sweets like cannoli for feasts and celebrations.
11. A short honest note: food history is full of stories that can't be proven. Families cooked differently from region to region and house to house. This is the general picture, not the one true version.
12. Payoff: their kitchen had three rules — buy simple, cook slow, waste nothing. Pick three dishes for this week: pasta e fagioli on Monday, peppers and eggs for lunch, a Sunday pot on the weekend.
13. Outro: Sal's Italian Kitchen for the Italian family dishes, and the Copycat Cookbook for the restaurant favorites that grew out of these kitchens (description). Tease the next video.
